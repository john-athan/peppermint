## 1. Why people stopped paying for local news

### The bundle broke

1. **Classifieds left.** Flats, cars, jobs, furniture moved to Kleinanzeigen, ImmoScout, StepStone. The paper lost the reason most households opened it on Saturday.
2. **Obituaries partly left.** Online memorial portals and funeral homes took a share of the most loyal reason to buy.
3. **TV listings, weather, cinema times, horoscopes, recipes** all became free apps. Each was a small reason to keep the subscription.
4. **Shop flyers** (Prospekte) moved to apps like kaufDA and Marktguru.
5. **Dating and greetings ads** moved to platforms.
6. **What was left is the expensive part** (reporting), sold alone, without the cheap useful parts that carried it.

### Price and money

7. **Too expensive for what it is.** Digital local subscriptions often cost 10 to 25 € a month after the trial, more than Netflix, for fewer pages than before.
8. **Prices anchored to print costs.** The digital price still pays for presses and delivery the reader never sees.
9. **The 1 € trial trap.** Cheap first months, then a jump. Readers learn to distrust the offer.
10. **Cancelling is hard.** Phone-only or letter-only cancellation, retention calls. Germany introduced the mandatory cancel button in 2022 partly because of this.
11. **Subscription fatigue.** Streaming, music, cloud, fitness. Local news is the first thing cut.
12. **Cost of living.** Rents and energy went up; a nice-to-have goes.
13. **"I already pay for news."** The Rundfunkbeitrag (18.36 € a month) feels like the news fee. Public broadcasters run free regional sites.
14. **Free substitutes everywhere.** Free weekly Anzeigenblätter, local radio, city hall's own channels.
15. **No way to pay small.** No single article, no week, no topic. All or nothing.
16. **Paywalls are easy to bypass.** Reader mode, archive sites, screenshots in WhatsApp groups.
17. **One person pays, everyone reads.** The screenshot in the parents' group serves forty households.
18. **Bundled with what I do not want.** ePaper, print, supplements, a sports package.
19. **Household inheritance ended.** People subscribed because their parents did. Young households never started.
20. **Willingness to pay is low in Germany anyway.** Around 13 % paid for any online news (Reuters Digital News Report 2024).

### The product got worse

21. **Fewer local reporters.** Newsrooms were cut for twenty years. Council meetings, courts and school boards go uncovered.
22. **Central desk content.** Large parts of a "local" paper come from a regional or national hub, same text in ten cities.
23. **Agency copy** (dpa) that every other site also has.
24. **Press release journalism.** Police reports and city hall statements reprinted with a new headline.
25. **SEO filler.** "Speed cameras in X today", "These shops are closing", written for Google, not for residents.
26. **Clickbait and teasers.** "You won't believe what happened on Hauptstraße."
27. **The paywall is on the wrong things.** Trivia behind the wall, important stories free, or the reverse with no logic the reader can follow.
28. **Too late.** Facebook groups and WhatsApp know about the fire an hour earlier.
29. **Too broad.** A county paper covers 40 towns; I care about my district.
30. **Too narrow in the wrong way.** Club anniversaries, golden weddings, rifle club festivals, written for a readership of seventy-year-olds.
31. **Too negative.** Crime, accidents, closures. Little that helps.
32. **Not useful.** It reports that the road is closed; it does not tell me how long, which detour, or whether my bus still runs.
33. **No follow-up.** A story about a planned building appears once and then nothing for three years.
34. **Errors and typos.** Fewer editors, more mistakes, few visible corrections.
35. **AI-generated filler** started to appear, and readers notice.
36. **No faces.** Readers do not know a single reporter by name any more.

### Trust and identity

37. **Too close to local power.** Perceived friendliness to the mayor, the savings bank, the big advertisers.
38. **Monopoly.** Many regions have one paper. No competition, no reason to try harder, no alternative voice.
39. **Owned by a chain.** A few large publishing groups own many titles. The paper feels like a branch office.
40. **Political distrust.** For some readers, "mainstream media" as a whole is the enemy.
41. **Bias complaints from both sides.** Too left for some, too establishment for others.
42. **Opinion mixed into reporting.** Readers cannot tell what is news and what is comment.
43. **Advertorials look like articles.** Readers feel tricked.
44. **Toxic comments.** The comment section drives people away or got switched off.

### The experience is bad

45. **No finished overview.** The website is an endless feed. You never know whether you have seen what matters today, so you never feel done.
46. **Websites full of ads, auto-play video, pop-ups.**
47. **Cookie and "pay or okay" banners** before the first sentence.
48. **Slow pages** from dozens of third-party scripts.
49. **ePaper is a PDF replica.** Pinch, zoom, scroll sideways on a phone.
50. **Account chaos.** Print customer number, separate web login, separate app login.
51. **Bad apps.** Crashes, stale content, no useful notifications.
52. **Push notifications that are not for me.** National breaking news from a local app.
53. **Cannot share a paid article** with a friend or neighbour.
54. **No search worth the name,** no archive access.
55. **Accessibility gaps.** Small type, no read-aloud, poor contrast.
56. **German only.** Newcomers who read English, Turkish, Arabic, Ukrainian, Polish are not served.

### Life changed

57. **People move more.** Renters, commuters, students. Less rooted, less interested in the town's politics.
58. **Live here, work there.** Attention splits across two places, loyalty to neither.
59. **News avoidance.** A large share of people avoid news on purpose because it is depressing or tiring.
60. **The habit formed on social media.** Under-35s get local information from Instagram, TikTok and WhatsApp channels.
61. **Facebook groups and Nextdoor** answer the everyday local questions for free and fast.
62. **City hall, police and fire brigade publish directly** on social media and their own sites.
63. **Influencers cover food and openings** better than the paper.
64. **AI answers.** Search summaries and chatbots answer "what happened in X" without a visit.
65. **Less club life.** Fewer people in Vereine, churches, parties, the networks the paper reported on.
66. **Time.** Twenty minutes with the morning paper are gone; attention is fragmented.
67. **Local news only matters when something happens to me,** and then I search once and leave.
68. **The value is invisible.** Accountability works quietly. Readers never see the scandal that did not happen because a reporter was in the room.
69. **Public good problem.** Everyone benefits from a watched city hall, so each person waits for someone else to pay.
70. **"Ads pay for that, right?"** Many people do not know the business model collapsed.
71. **Gifted digital access feels like nothing.** There is no object, no ritual, so it feels like it costs nothing to make.

## 2. Why people still come to local news

### The overview

1. **Knowing what is going on in the region.** The most common answer to "why do you read it?". Not one topic, the whole picture.
2. **A finished edition.** Read it, put it down, done. Informed without scrolling forever.
3. **Not missing what everyone will talk about** at work, at the bakery, at the club.
4. **The small towns around.** What happens in the next village matters too: the bypass, the hospital, the school everyone's kids go to.

### Things that change my day

5. **Road closures, construction, detours.**
6. **Public transport disruption,** strikes, replacement buses.
7. **Weather extremes,** floods, storms, heat warnings, school cancellations.
8. **Water, power, gas outages.**
9. **Waste collection changes,** recycling centre hours.
10. **Emergency pharmacy and doctor on duty.**
11. **Parking rules, fees, residents' permits.**

### "What was that?"

12. **Sirens, helicopters, smoke.** Blue-light news is the most-read local category almost everywhere.
13. **Police operations in my street.**
14. **Missing persons, lost pets, poison bait warnings for dogs.**
15. **Rumour check.** "Someone in the group says the school is closing. True?"

### Home and money

16. **Rents and housing,** new building areas, development plans next to my house.
17. **Property tax, waste fees, water prices.**
18. **Who builds what across the street.** Building permits, cranes, demolitions.
19. **Local prices and deals,** markets, flyers.
20. **Jobs nearby.**
21. **Business openings and closings.** "What is opening in the old bakery?"

### Family

22. **Schools,** catchment areas, new buildings, teacher shortages, results.
23. **Kita places** and waiting lists.
24. **Playgrounds, swimming pools, youth clubs.**
25. **Children's sports,** amateur league results, seeing your kid's name or photo.
26. **Health care,** hospital closures, doctor shortages, new practices.
27. **Care homes** for parents.

### People

28. **Obituaries.** Who died, when the funeral is, where to send condolences.
29. **Births, weddings, anniversaries.**
30. **People I know** in the news.
31. **Gossip and conversation currency.** Something to talk about at the bakery.
32. **Local heroes,** volunteers, firefighters, club founders.

### Going out

33. **What is on this weekend.** Markets, festivals, concerts, Christmas markets.
34. **Restaurant openings and reviews.**
35. **Cinema, theatre, exhibitions.**
36. **Local sports,** the town's football, basketball or hockey club.

### Power and politics

37. **Council decisions** that cost me money or change my street.
38. **Local elections,** candidates, results, referendums.
39. **Scandals and investigations.** Accountability stories still drive the biggest subscription spikes.
40. **Big projects,** the new stadium, the tram line, the closed swimming pool.
41. **Court cases** with local relevance.
42. **Planning disputes,** trees felled, parks built over.

### Belonging

43. **Heimat.** Identity, the feeling of knowing where you live.
44. **Nostalgia and history.** Old photos, "what used to be here".
45. **Newcomers** learning how the town works.
46. **People who moved away** following their hometown.
47. **The morning ritual** for older readers.
48. **Puzzles, crosswords, sudoku.**
49. **Letters to the editor,** being heard.

### Trust

50. **Local news is more trusted than national news** in most surveys.
51. **Verification** against rumours in social media groups.
52. **A named reporter** you can call.
53. **Crisis.** During COVID, local rules and incidence numbers drove record local subscriptions. In a crisis, people want the local, checked answer.

### Professional need

54. **Local business owners** watching competitors, permits, city plans.
55. **Estate agents, developers, architects** watching development plans.
56. **Lawyers, tax advisers** watching courts and fees.
57. **Politicians and their staff,** associations, unions.
58. **Teachers, doctors, social workers** watching their sector locally.
59. **Companies relocating staff** who need to orient them.

## 3. What the two lists say together

- **The first reason is the overview.** Asked why they read the paper, most people say some version of "to know what is going on in the region". Not one topic, the whole picture, edited, and finished. A product that starts from the street or from a feed misses the main job.
- **People did not stop needing local information.** They stopped needing a newspaper to get it. Almost every reason in list 2 is still alive; most are now served badly, by Facebook groups, city hall PDFs and rumour.
- **The paper lost the cheap useful parts and kept the expensive part.** It now sells reporting alone, at a high price, in a bad wrapper.
- **Coverage of the small towns collapsed first.** The county edition shrank to the district town; thirty villages got a correspondent paid by the line, then nobody.
- **What people come for is mostly service, safety, money, family and people.** Accountability matters enormously but is invisible until it is not. A sustainable product sells the visible value and funds the invisible one.
- **Speed and verification are a pair.** Social media is fast and wrong; the paper is slow and right. The gap between them is the product.
- **Some readers pay a lot, most pay nothing.** Businesses and professionals have real budgets; households have very little. One price for everyone leaves money on both ends.
- **Trust is local and personal.** Faces, corrections, transparency about money and about who owns the paper.

So the thesis: **rebuild the bundle around the region, with an edited daily overview at its centre, made by people who live in the towns they cover, and owned by the region itself.**

## 4. The product

### 4.1 Five layers

1. **Der Tag in der Region (free, no account).** Every morning at 6: 10 to 15 items chosen and ranked by the editor on duty, each a headline and two sentences, every town of the region in it when something happened there. It ends: "Das war's für heute." Six minutes, then you know what is going on. Also as email, as a 5-minute audio briefing, and as a printed weekly for readers who want paper. This is the front page and the habit.
2. **My street and What was that? (free with an account).** Alerts for one address: road works, building applications, outages, council decisions, police operations. Verified answers to sirens and rumours within 20 minutes, pushed only to accounts nearby.
3. **The full stories (membership).** Every item in the daily edition links to the full story: background, what it means for you, sources, follow-ups tracked to the end. Members also choose a home town and get its own section.
4. **The region's memory and community (membership).** Searchable archive, "what used to be here" map with old photos, moderated forum with real names, verified events calendar, obituaries and memorial pages.
5. **Rathaus Pro (professional subscription).** Every council meeting in the region transcribed, summarised and searchable; keyword and address alerts on agendas, building applications, tenders, commercial register entries; export for teams.

### 4.2 Who supplies the information

| Who | How many (year 3) | What they do | How they are paid |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Core newsroom** | Editor in chief plus 6 | The daily edition, the city, verification, investigations, legal responsibility, editing every correspondent story | Salary at or above the regional collective agreement |
| **Local correspondents** | 12, each covering 2 to 3 of the 30 towns | Their towns: councils, schools, clubs, openings, people. Their face is on their section | Retainer 1,000 € a month for about 12 to 15 hours a week, plus 35 % of the fee of every member who chose one of their towns, plus 20 % of business profiles they sign in their towns |
| **Meeting watchers** | About 40 residents | Attend council and committee meetings without a livestream, file structured notes | Around 50 € per meeting |
| **Clubs, schools, parishes, city halls** | Open | Their own announcements, labelled "Mitteilung" and visibly separate from reporting | Free, it brings readers |
| **Public data** | 20 to 30 feeds | Building applications, council agendas, police and fire reports, outages, tenders, court lists | Automated; a human checks before anything goes out |

**Why a hybrid, and not a platform of independent creators.**
- Someone has to stand behind every story: German press law asks for a responsible editor, the right of reply, corrections. A central desk carries that; thirty independent creators cannot.
- Readers pay for one trusted edition, not for thirty newsletters.
- Creators drift to the towns and topics that pay. The salaried core and the retainer guarantee that the smallest town's council is covered too.
- The correspondents still get what makes creators work: their own face and section, a direct line to their readers, and income that grows with their town.

**How correspondents are recruited:** local people who already do it badly paid or for free: line-paid freelancers of the old paper, club press officers, retired teachers and civil servants, journalism students from the region. Two weeks of training, a style and ethics guide, a desk editor as their contact, every story edited before it runs.

### 4.3 The engine behind it

- **Public data intake.** Council information systems (most German municipalities run ALLRIS or SessionNet), building applications, police and fire press releases, transport feeds, outage pages, court lists, tenders, commercial register. Pulled automatically, matched to addresses and towns.
- **Meeting coverage at scale.** Livestreams and recordings get transcribed and summarised by AI; a human checks each summary before it is published; meeting watchers cover the rest.
- **The desk builds the daily edition** from correspondent stories, newsroom stories and checked data items. Ranking is an editor's decision, never an algorithm's.
- **Everything is labelled.** Reported by a person, written by AI and checked, announcement by an organisation, advertising.

### 4.4 Product rules

- No third-party tracking, so no consent banner. Contextual ads only, first-party, labelled, at most one per page for non-members.
- Pages load in under one second on a mid-range phone.
- The daily edition is finite. No endless feed anywhere in the product.
- Cancel with two clicks. Pause instead of cancel. No price jumps after trials.
- Every journalist and correspondent has a face, a beat, a direct line and a public corrections log.
- Gift links: members can send any article free to anyone, five per month.
- Read-aloud on every article. Key service information translated into English, Turkish, Arabic, Ukrainian, with a human check.
- Ownership, finances and the correspondent payout published once a year.

- **Pro seats.** Validate with 20 paid pilots before building more.

## 7. Ownership and profit

### 7.1 Who owns peppermint

A **registered cooperative (eG)**, owned by the region.

- **Three groups of co-owners.** Readers who buy at least one share; everyone who works there, staff and correspondents; investing members (local businesses, angels, foundations) who bring capital.
- **One co-owner, one vote** in the general assembly, regardless of how many shares. Investing members' votes are capped by statute, so money cannot buy control.
- **Supervisory board** of six: three elected by readers, two by staff, one by correspondents. It appoints and controls the management board.
- **Editorial statute.** The newsroom confirms the editor in chief with a two-thirds vote. No member, board, sponsor or business customer may instruct coverage. The statute can only be changed with the newsroom's consent.
- **It cannot be sold.** A cooperative's members would have to vote to dissolve or convert it; there is no owner who can sell to a chain.

Why a cooperative: it is the strongest answer to "too close to power" and to chain ownership, and buying a share is a stronger tie than a subscription. taz shows that it works in German media. The costs: raising money is slower, every cooperative must pay for a mandatory audit, and members can withdraw their shares after a notice period, so the statute sets that period at five years.

The alternative, if classic investors are needed at the start: a GmbH in steward ownership. Investors get a capped return, voting rights stay with the people working there, and the company cannot be sold.

### 7.2 Where the money goes

**Every member's 6 €**, for a member whose home town is covered by a correspondent: 2.10 € to that correspondent, 3.90 € to the newsroom, the product and running costs. For members in the city, the full 6 € goes to the core newsroom's budget.

**Operating profit, year 3 (253k €), in this order:**

| Step | Share | Year 3 |
|---|---|---|
| Profit pool for everyone who works there, split by hours worked | 10 % | 25k € |
| Statutory reserve (cooperatives must build one) | 20 % | 51k € |
| Dividend on shares, capped at 2 % a year | at most 2 % of share capital | 12k € on 600k € reader shares |
| Reinvested: new correspondents, new towns, investigations | rest | 165k € |

Correspondents earn three ways: retainer, member share, business commission. In year 3 that is about 19k € a year for a part-time role of 12 to 15 hours a week, far above the line fees the old paper paid, and it grows with the town.

**Never tied to clicks.** Nobody's pay depends on page views. Member share follows the home town readers chose, the profit pool follows hours worked, bonuses for staff follow member retention and correction quality.

## 8. Go to market

1. **Start with the city plus five towns,** with five correspondents already in place.
2. **Lead with the daily edition.** Free, by email and in the app, from day one. Promote it with the line people already use: "Wissen, was in der Region los ist. Sechs Minuten am Morgen."
3. **Sell cooperative shares before launch.** 3,000 founding co-owners at 100 € and up fund the start and become the first readers and ambassadors.
4. **Be fastest and right on blue light.** Every siren answered in 20 minutes earns trust.
5. **My street door hangers** in new-build areas: "3 building applications within 200 m of your door this year. Get the next one."
6. **Correspondents in their towns:** club meetings, school councils, the bakery. They sign up readers and businesses; they earn from both.
7. **Welcome kit for newcomers** with registration offices, landlords and employers; three months of membership paid by the employer.
8. **Funeral home partnerships** for memorial pages.
9. **Rathaus Pro door to door:** estate agents, architects, law firms, party offices, each demo on their own address.
10. **A printed weekly** for older readers, from year 2, distributed with the daily edition's best stories.

## 10. Risks

| Risk | Mitigation |
|---|---|
| Correspondent quality varies | Training, style guide, every story edited, desk editor per cluster, two trained backups per cluster |
| A correspondent becomes too close to their town's mayor or clubs | Conflict-of-interest declaration, rotation of council coverage every two years, the desk covers conflicts directly |
| City data is incomplete or locked | Start with towns on ALLRIS or SessionNet; freedom-of-information requests; meeting watchers fill gaps |
| AI summary errors | Human check before publishing, labelled, public corrections log, error rate as a KPI |
| Incumbent paper copies the edition and alerts | It cannot drop its print cost base, its ad tech or its chain owner; the region owning peppermint is the moat |
| Conflict of interest from Pro and business revenue | Editorial statute, published customer and sponsor list, no business can buy coverage or silence |
| Cooperative raises money slowly | Development loan and investing members as second pillar; GmbH in steward ownership as fallback |
| Members withdraw shares | Five-year notice period in the statute; reserve built from year 3 |
| Membership ramps slower | 70 % of revenue does not depend on it; cut marketing before newsroom |
| Platform dependence (WhatsApp, Instagram) | Social only for discovery; the edition, the account and email carry the relationship |
| Legal (press law, labelling, GDPR) | Responsible editor for all content, labelled ads (§22 MStV), no tracking, press law counsel on retainer |

## 11. Beyond one region

- **A federation of regional cooperatives.** Each region owns its own peppermint; a shared service cooperative owns the software, the training and national sponsorship sales. The model of the cooperative banks: local ownership, shared infrastructure.
- **Region two costs about half of region one,** because the engine, the editing tools and the correspondent training already exist.
- **Sell the engine to existing publishers.** Meeting transcription, address alerts and the edition builder are useful to regional papers too; a B2B line that can fund the newsroom if the reader side ramps slowly.
- **Neighbouring products.** Regional vouchers that keep spending local, a neighbourhood marketplace, data dashboards for the region's own administrations under a strict firewall.
